# What actually produces followers — Instagram, 19 August 2026

*Rewritten 8:35pm after Mark supplied the missing piece. The earlier version of

## Conclusion

**Follows track what the piece asks the viewer to become, not how well the piece
performs.** Pieces that present the publication and its people convert at
2.5–3%. Pieces that promote a podcast episode convert at 0–0.5% — even when
Instagram distributes them well and the audience plainly enjoys them.

Pooled: **podcast-episode reels 2 follows from 656 viewers (0.30%)** against
**everything else 61 from 2,082 (2.93%)** — a **9.6× gap**, Fisher two-tailed
**p = 8.6 × 10⁻⁶**. At the rate the other pieces achieved, the two podcast reels
should have produced **19 follows. They produced 2.**

## Why the earlier conclusion was wrong

I concluded at 8:22pm that **format** decided it: carousel stays in-network and
converts, reel gets pushed to strangers and doesn't. Three points fitted that
line perfectly. The fourth destroys it — because **the best performer of all four
is a reel** (3.01%). A reel converted 6× better than another reel. Format cannot
be the variable when the variable takes both values on the same side.

What I actually had was a confound: the only non-reel in the set was also the
only piece that wasn't about a podcast episode. Mark sending the wrong screenshot
is what exposed it. One more observation collapsed a clean-looking story.

## What the new piece establishes

**Instagram liked the podcast reel. People liked it. Nobody followed.**
Skip rate 43.5% (below benchmark — good), like rate 7.6%, save rate 1.2%, share
rate 0.7% — all flagged *higher* than benchmark by Instagram. 8s average watch on
a 16s reel is 50% completion. It reached **395 viewers, more than the carousel's
322**, and 87.2% of them were non-followers. It converted 2.

This is the finding that matters: **engagement and acquisition are decoupled.**
The podcast reel out-engaged the carousel on every signal Instagram optimises for
and under-acquired it 5:1. Judging content on likes would have picked exactly the
wrong piece.

**The funnel breaks at the first step, not the second.**

| | viewer → profile visit | profile visit → follow |
|---|---|---|
| Alessia carousel | **4.35%** | 57% |
| Alessia podcast reel | **1.27%** | 40% |

Once someone reaches the profile they convert at a respectable rate either way.
The podcast reel simply doesn't send them there. A viewer who enjoys an episode
teaser has been given a reason to *listen*, not a reason to *subscribe to a
publication*.

**Comments versus saves is the tell.** Carousel: 7 comments, 0 saves, 0 shares.
Podcast reel: 0 comments, 5 saves, 3 shares. Saves and shares are consumption
intent — *I want this later*. Comments are relationship intent — *I want to be in
this conversation*. Hypothesis, not fact, but the split is clean and comment
count is the cheaper thing to watch than follows.

## What this changes in practice

1. **Recommendation withdrawn.** The earlier note said to run the Zoe podcast set
   as a carousel instead of a reel. **That will not fix it.** The container was
   never the problem. Running the same episode promo as a carousel would plausibly
   move it from 0.0% to ~0.5%, not to 2.5%.
2. **Stop loading acquisition onto podcast promos.** They are credibility and
   retention assets — they show the publication does real editorial work. Make
   them, keep them cheap, and do not measure them on follows.
3. **Put the acquisition load on publication-and-people pieces.** Both 2.5%+
   performers showed the masthead and the roster, not a single episode. That is
   the format worth repeating on a schedule.
4. **The testable rewrite:** lead a podcast promo with the *map* and the *teacher's
   place in it*, and mention the episode second. If the proposition is what moves
   the number, that piece should land nearer 2.5% than 0.5%. Worth one attempt
   with Steph, Rayne or Ryan — a Melbourne audience, so geography isn't confounded.
5. **Judge every piece on profile-visits-per-viewer.** It moves first, it moves on
   smaller numbers than follows, and it is where the failure actually occurs.

**Top sources of views for Alessia's podcast reel** (Overview tab, scroll past
"What affects your views"). Mark's screenshot stopped short of it.

- If it shows **substantial Reels tab + Explore**, the reel got cold reach and

- If it shows **Feed and Profile only**, like the carousel, then both Alessia
  pieces were in-network and the difference is something else, and the whole

That single panel decides between the two surviving explanations. Everything
above is written as though the first case holds, because 87.2% non-followers
makes it likelier — but it is **not yet established**.

## Assumptions flagged

- **Measured:** every figure is from Instagram Insights or GA4 screenshots taken
  by Mark on 18–19 Aug 2026, and frame-level story insights at ~13h age.
- **Not like-for-like on totals:** the pieces are 13, 10, 9 days and 9 hours old
  respectively. All but Zoe's have plateaued, so the *rates* compare even though
  the totals are not final. Zoe's rate could still improve.
- **Small numbers.** 2 follows and 0 follows are individually near-meaningless.
  The pooled comparison (2/656 vs 61/2,082, p = 8.6 × 10⁻⁶) is what carries the
  weight, and it rests on the grouping being the right one — see the open question
  above.
- **n = 4.** One further piece has already overturned this analysis once today.
  Treat the conclusion as the current best reading, not as settled.
