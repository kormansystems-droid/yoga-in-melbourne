# Yoga in Melbourne: retreat venue brokerage

**Date:** 7 September 2026
**Status:** Open option, explored in discussion. **No decision taken and nothing built.**
Written so the reasoning survives the session and can be re-examined.
**Origin:** Mark was looking at `jungleyoga.com` and asked whether an Australian equivalent
could run off the back of the YiM teacher engine.

---

## 1. The conclusion

**The idea worth keeping is not an Australian Jungle Yoga.** It is a **venue brokerage**: YiM
represents Australian venues and caterers, and sells packaged retreat capacity to yoga teachers
who already trust it.

Three reasons it is more interesting than the operator version:

1. **It is firewall-compatible.** The teacher is the *customer*, not the supplier. Structurally
   identical to studio subscriptions: infrastructure and presence, never coverage. Venues and
   caterers never appear in editorial, so that side is clean.
2. **It monetises teacher trust, not consumer audience.** Teacher trust exists now. Consumer
   audience does not. This is available roughly a year earlier than retreat referral commission.
3. **It is national from day one.** Editorial does not scale across cities cheaply; a venue
   network does, because a Melbourne teacher will happily hold a retreat in Byron or Tasmania.

**The product is retreat management, not venue introduction, and that is what makes it a
business.** Teachers hate managing infrastructure (5c). Selling relief rather than information
roughly doubles the fee, makes the relationship recurring instead of one-time, and is the first
version of this idea that reaches six figures. It also makes the founder the capacity ceiling.

**The top risk is therefore willingness to pay**, at roughly 27% of a teacher's retreat margin.
Not competition, not filling, and no longer disintermediation. Test it with a priced offer, not
a conversation. See sections 5c, 7 and 8.

**Founder-market fit is unusually strong, and it changes the sequencing advice given elsewhere
in this session.** Mark lived in Byron for thirteen years and is well connected to caterers and
service providers there. The business sits on the intersection of two networks he already owns:
**Melbourne teacher relationships on the demand side, Byron supplier relationships on the supply
side.** Most people attempting this have one or the other. See section 5a.

---

## 2. What Jungle Yoga actually is

Read 7 Sep 2026. Not a retreat operator hiring teachers, which was the initial assumption and
was wrong. It is **infrastructure for teachers who bring their own guests**: three Thai
locations (Koh Samui, Khao Sok, Chiang Mai), handling accommodation, meals, transport,
equipment, excursions and onsite support. Tagline: "You focus on your guests, we handle the
rest." No pricing disclosed.

The teacher is their customer. That is the shape worth copying, not the venue ownership.

## 3. The four layers, and where YiM can stand

| Layer | Needs | YiM has it? |
|---|---|---|
| Editorial retreat calendar | Publishing, teacher relationships | Yes |
| **Venue brokerage (this note)** | **Venue supply, teacher trust** | **Partly** |
| Referral commission on teachers' retreats | Consumer audience, attribution | Not yet |
| Marketplace holding funds | Travel-money compliance, trust accounts | No |
| Operator owning the retreat | Venue capital, local ops, insurance, liability | No |

**The operator layer was considered and rejected.** If YiM runs retreats and hires teachers,
YiM is covering its own suppliers, and a reader can no longer tell whether a teacher is on the
front page because her story is good or because she is on the October roster. That is the one
expansion that puts the moat and the revenue in direct opposition.

## 5. Geography: the demand side is the expansion that counts

**Australia-wide teachers: viable, but not under this masthead.** "Yoga in Melbourne" pitching a
venue to a Brisbane teacher is a category error. A national customer base needs its own brand,
and what transfers from YiM is not the name but Mark's relationships and a track record.

**The mechanism is referral, not cold sales and not editorial replication.** Yoga teacher
communities are small and highly connected: training cohorts, festival circuits, the same twenty
people in every 200-hour intake. Ten excellent Melbourne retreats will be talked about in Sydney
and Perth without being marketed there. That preserves the trust mechanism instead of spending
it, and it costs nothing. It also means **the first twelve months is a Melbourne business
regardless of ambition**, because referral needs completed retreats before it has anything to
say.

**Overseas teachers: expected to fail, on structure rather than execution.** A teacher fills
from her own student base, and her students are in her city. Selling London students an
Australian retreat adds long-haul airfare to an already $2,400 trip, roughly doubling it, and
break-even is around eight guests. Against Portugal or Morocco she will not reach eight. The
flow runs the other way: Australians fly to Bali because it is cheap and close; nobody flies to
Australia because it is cheap.

**The exception is Singapore, and it is not a narrow one.** Mark pushed back on this and was
right; the argument above was over-generalised from the London case.

**Singapore is a 100% export retreat market, structurally and permanently.**
BookYogaRetreats lists **zero yoga retreats inside Singapore**, against **Bali 281, Thailand
159, Sri Lanka 79** (read 7 Sep 2026). The country is 730 square kilometres and fully
urbanised, so there is no domestic capacity to build. Every retreat a Singaporean teacher runs
is offshore, by necessity rather than preference.

Why Australia can win a share of that against the Bali default:

* **Climate is the product.** Singapore is hot and humid every day of the year, and so is Bali.
  Cool, dry, seasonal Australia is genuinely novel rather than marginally different. For a
  Singaporean buyer that is the reason to travel, not a detail.
* **Flight cost does not dominate**, unlike Europe or the US. Singapore to eastern Australia is
  a premium over Bali, not a doubling of trip cost. **Brisbane is the practical gateway** for
  Byron and Noosa; see the destination section below.
* **Differentiation.** Bali is saturated; an Australian retreat is a status product in a way
  another Ubud week is not.

**This inverts one of the assumptions above.** Trust falls with distance, but *value* rises with
it. A Melbourne teacher can drive to Daylesford and look at the venue herself, so brokerage is a
convenience. A Singaporean teacher cannot scout a Tasmanian property, so someone who has walked
it is genuinely valuable. Information asymmetry is highest exactly where the relationship is
weakest. Which of those dominates is the open question, not a settled one.

**Perth as the gateway was my inference and Mark corrected it. The prime destinations are
Byron, Noosa and Tasmania.** The error is worth recording: I optimised flight hours, the
variable I could reason about from a desk, and ignored the one that actually decides a premium
leisure purchase. Margaret River is a wine region; Byron is a yoga region with international
recognition and existing venue stock. **Destination brand and venue supply beat flight time**,
and a place with no yoga reputation is not a destination however close it is.

Access from Singapore, corrected: **there is no direct Singapore to Gold Coast service**
(verified 7 Sep 2026). **Brisbane is the gateway** for both Byron and Noosa, direct from
Singapore, then ground transfer. Tasmania needs a domestic connection on top.

The three destinations do different jobs:

| Destination | Role | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Byron** | The default, and where the premium residence stock actually is. International recognition, deepest agent inventory, lowest sales friction | Most competitive on the surface, though see the supply-pool correction below: the good houses are not on any retreat platform |
| **Noosa** | Premium Australian holiday brand, subtropical, agent stock in the hinterland and on the coast | Middle on both counts |
| **Tasmania** | The differentiated one. Cool climate, wilderness, food and wine. Genuinely unlike Bali, which is what the Singapore case turns on | Hardest to reach, market has to be created, premium residence stock unverified |

### The supply pool is not the one the retreat platforms index

**This is the most important correction in the note, and it came from Mark.** The premium Byron
supply is **not** purpose-built retreat centres. Byron Yoga Centre is a basic facility with an
established yoga school attached, and is inappropriate for this product. **The best properties
are palatial private residences let through luxury short-stay agents such as Luxico.**

Consequences, and they are large:

1. **The retreat platforms do not compete for this segment at all.** Retreat Guru, RetreatHub and
   BookYogaRetreats index purpose-built retreat centres. Luxury short-stay residences sit in a
   completely different inventory pool and appear on none of them. The competitive analysis in
   section 7 applies to the mid-market, not to this.
2. **My "Byron venues are easy to find" claim was wrong**, and wrong because I read listing
   platforms and mistook their index for the market.
3. **Supply is concentrated, which is good.** Luxico alone covers Byron (Wategos, Bilongil,
   Bangalow, Suffolk Park), Sunshine Coast, Gold Coast, Mornington Peninsula, NSW South Coast,
   Sydney and the Whitsundays (site read 7 Sep 2026). Three to five agency relationships would
   give national premium inventory, rather than cold-calling individual properties.
4. **They already permit and service this use.** Luxico offers "Location Hire" for corporate
   events and shoots, and concierge including private chefs and "wellness experiences". The
   feared blocker, that luxury holiday lets prohibit groups, does not apply to them.

### So what does YiM actually add?

Not operations, since Luxico already has chefs and concierge. **YiM brings the teacher**, and
the agents' pain is midweek and shoulder-season voids. A four-night midweek group in May is
exactly the fill they want. The pitch is a straight channel-partner one: *we bring you group
bookings in your empty weeks.*

And one piece of knowledge nobody else holds: **which of these houses you can actually teach
twelve people in.** A palatial residence has no shala. It needs roughly 60 to 70 square metres
of flat, uncarpeted, well-lit space, indoor or covered deck, with the right morning aspect and
acoustics. No listing photo tells you that, and no agent thinks in those terms. **This is the
real product, and it is why walking the properties is not marketing but the actual service.**

### 5a. Founder-market fit, and the advice it overturns

Mark lived in Byron for **thirteen years** and is well connected to caterers and other service
providers there. Three consequences.

**1. The moat I described as needing to be built already exists.** The service is knowing which
palatial residences you can actually teach twelve people in, which agents deliver, and which
caterers turn up. That is thirteen years of local knowledge, and it cannot be acquired by a
competitor at any speed that matters.

**2. The supply-side cold start largely disappears.** Assembling venue and catering supply was
the main practical obstacle in this note. Warm relationships remove most of it.

**3. It reverses the sequencing advice given twice in this session.** The repeated conclusion
was *wait, build the audience first, do not monetise before the asset exists.* That is right for
retreat referral commission, which needs a consumer audience. **It is wrong here.** This business
runs on teacher trust and Byron supplier relationships, and neither depends on the editorial
audience. The constraint is Mark's time, not YiM's maturity.

**It also narrows the geography, in the opposite direction to section 5's earlier conclusion.**
That section argued Tasmania was where the defensible position lay, because the stock is
obscure. But defensibility from personal knowledge exists only where the personal knowledge is.
**Byron is the moat; Tasmania would be a cold start.** Concentrate on Byron. Noosa and Tasmania
are later expansions and should be costed as such, not treated as equivalent launch options.

**The open question is recency.** Thirteen years in Byron is decisive if the connections are
current, and much less so if he left a decade ago. Byron has turned over heavily since 2020:
property ownership, operators and prices have all moved. Worth being honest with himself about
which relationships he could call tomorrow and which are memories. It is also worth checking
whether the connections extend to the **luxury residence agents** specifically, or mainly to
caterers and service providers, since the agents are the harder half.

### Revised economics for this segment

Whole-house rates rather than per-person retreat-centre rates. Estimates, not quotes:

| Configuration | House | Catering | Total spend | Broker at 10% | at 12.5% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 pax, 4 nights, $2,500/night | $10,000 | $10,800 | $20,800 | $2,080 | $2,600 |
| 12 pax, 4 nights, $4,000/night | $16,000 | $13,200 | $29,200 | $2,920 | $3,650 |
| 14 pax, 5 nights, $3,000/night | $15,000 | $16,800 | $31,800 | $3,180 | $3,975 |

The teacher sells 12 places at $3,000 to $4,500, so $36,000 to $54,000 of revenue against a
$20,000 to $30,000 cost base. **She can comfortably absorb a broker margin**, which the
mid-market model could not support as easily.

At roughly $2,000 per retreat brokered: 10 retreats $20,000; 20 retreats $40,000; 35 retreats
$70,000; 50 retreats $100,000. **Deal size roughly doubles versus the regional-Victoria model
in section 4**, and the buyer is far less price-sensitive.

Practical implication: **the premium residence segment, not the retreat-centre segment, is the
business.** Byron is where that supply and that demand already exist, so it is the right place
to start after all, for the opposite of the reason given earlier in this section.

**What it does not change:** YiM has no presence, brand or relationships in Singapore, and the
trust mechanism that makes the Melbourne version work is entirely absent there. The likely route
in is a beachhead teacher with an existing Australian connection, of whom there are many given
study and family ties, or a local partner.

Still expected to fail on flight economics: **Europe and North America.** Long-haul roughly
doubles a $2,400 trip against a break-even of about eight guests, and Portugal or Morocco wins.
Also worth serving opportunistically: **New Zealand and Hong Kong**, and teachers **already
travelling to Australia** to teach or train, for whom a retreat is an add-on rather than the
reason to come.

### 5b. Matched sizing, which largely dissolves the fill objection

Mark's point: **property size flexes to the size and budget of the group.** This is not a
detail, it removes the break-even cliff that section 7 was built around.

Whole-house rates do not scale linearly with bed count, so **per-head cost is close to flat
across group sizes**. Estimated Byron rates, four nights, private chef at $180 per head per day:

| Pax | $/night | House | Catering | Total spend | **Per head** | Broker at 10% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | $1,400 | $5,600 | $5,400 | $11,000 | **$1,833** | $1,100 |
| 8 | $1,900 | $7,600 | $7,200 | $14,800 | **$1,850** | $1,480 |
| 10 | $2,300 | $9,200 | $9,000 | $18,200 | **$1,820** | $1,820 |
| 12 | $2,800 | $11,200 | $10,800 | $22,000 | **$1,833** | $2,200 |
| 16 (5n) | $4,200 | $21,000 | $17,280 | $38,280 | $2,392 | $3,828 |

At $3,200 a head retail the teacher makes **roughly 43% margin at 6, 8, 10 and 12 people**:
$8,200 on six, $16,400 on twelve. **There is no minimum viable audience.** A teacher who can
find six students has a profitable retreat, and that is nearly every working teacher rather than
only those with a following.

Note the 16-person row: margin drops to 25% because very large luxury houses are scarce and
priced at a premium. **The sweet spot looks like 10 to 12**, not the largest group that will fit.

**What it costs.** Broker revenue is not size-invariant. Six people pays $1,100 against $2,200
for twelve, for the same amount of work. Effort per deal is roughly constant, revenue is not,
which is the classic services trap.

Two responses, both structural:

1. **Charge a minimum fee**, around $1,200, with the percentage applying above that. Small deals
   should not be loss-making on time.
2. **Treat a small first retreat as customer acquisition, not as the transaction.** A teacher
   who succeeds with six runs ten next year and fourteen after that. The lifetime value is
   several times the first deal, which means **retention is the business and the introduction is
   not**. It also sharpens section 6: if you are only an introduction service she leaves after
   deal one, so being useful at each step up is what you are actually selling.

**One trap for the pilot.** Matched sizing makes a small pilot much easier to fill, and a
six-person success is a signal rather than a proof. It does not establish that the model earns
enough per deal to be worth Mark's time. Run the test at two sizes, or read a small win as
encouragement rather than validation.

### 5c. The demand thesis, and what it does to the business shape

Mark's position, from experience: **the vast majority of teachers hate managing infrastructure.
They love teaching and are happy to farm out the rest at a fee.**

If that holds, it is the most consequential statement in this note, because it changes what is
being sold.

**It largely kills the disintermediation risk that section 7 had just promoted to number one.**
Disintermediation happens when the value is an *introduction*: once she knows the house and the
caterer, she does not need you again. But if what she is buying is **not having to manage it**,
she needs you every time. The house being the same house is irrelevant. She still does not want
to make the twelve phone calls, chase the dietaries, or handle the transfer that goes wrong.

**Value shifts from information, which leaks and is consumed once, to relief, which is recurring
by nature.** That is a materially better business and it is the strongest argument yet in favour.

**But it changes the shape, and the cost should be seen clearly.** This is no longer brokerage,
it is **retreat management**. Effort per deal goes up rather than down, the founder becomes the
capacity constraint, and it drifts toward the principal end of the agent/principal line in
section 6, which raises the Australian Consumer Law exposure. Managing without contracting is
possible but needs care and advice.

**The fee changes with it.** Not 10% of venue spend but a management fee, and events-industry
norms run 15 to 25% of budget:

| Pax | Total spend | Her revenue | Her margin | Fee at 20% | As % of her margin | She keeps |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | $11,000 | $19,200 | $8,200 | $2,200 | 27% | $6,000 |
| 8 | $14,800 | $25,600 | $10,800 | $2,960 | 27% | $7,840 |
| 10 | $18,200 | $32,000 | $13,800 | $3,640 | 26% | $10,160 |
| 12 | $22,000 | $38,400 | $16,400 | $4,400 | 27% | $12,000 |

**Roughly double the brokerage fee, and it holds at about 27% of her margin across all sizes.**
That is affordable but highly visible: she is handing over a quarter of her profit and she will
feel it every time. Which is the crux, see below.

**Capacity becomes the ceiling instead of demand.** At a $4,000 average fee:

| Hours per retreat | 600 hrs/year available | 1,000 hrs/year |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | 50 retreats, $200,000 | 83 retreats, $332,000 |
| 20 | 30 retreats, $120,000 | 50 retreats, $200,000 |
| 30 | 20 retreats, $80,000 | 33 retreats, $132,000 |

**This is the first version of the idea that reaches six figures**, and it does so because the
fee doubled and retention became structural. It is also the first version that needs staff to
grow beyond the founder, at which point it is an agency with payroll rather than a side
business. Worth knowing before, not after.

### The challenge to the thesis, which is the thing to test

**"Teachers hate infrastructure" is the load-bearing assumption of the entire business and it is
currently unverified.** It is also the kind of claim that is true in the abstract and false at
the price. Ask any teacher *"would you like someone else to handle the logistics?"* and every
one says yes. Ask *"would you pay $4,000 of your $16,000 profit for it?"* and the answer is a
real number rather than a sentiment.

**So the test in section 8 must quote the fee.** A package without a price tests enthusiasm. A
package with the management fee stated tests the business. Do not let a warm response to the
first be mistaken for evidence of the second.

## 6. Two structural decisions that matter more than anything else

**1. Agent, never principal.** Introduce, and let the contract sit teacher-to-venue. The moment
YiM books the venue and resells it, it carries deposits, cancellation risk and Australian
Consumer Law exposure on a packaged product, and the operator risk rejected in section 3 returns
through the side door. *Needs actual legal advice before any money moves; this note is not it.*

**1a. Charge a minimum fee, not a bare percentage.** Matched sizing (section 5b) means small
groups at the same effort as large ones. A floor of roughly $1,200 with the percentage applying
above it keeps a six-person retreat from being loss-making on time.

**2. Charge a fee or subscription, not booking commission.** Retreat venues are repeat-use: a
teacher who finds a property she likes rebooks it directly for a decade, and YiM earns once.
RetreatHub's flat EUR 199 with explicitly no commission is not a pricing preference, it is a
confession that the booking cut cannot be tracked or enforced. Assume the same leak.

## 7. The risk that most likely kills it

**The risk ranking changed after section 5b.** Filling was the headline risk in the first
version of this note. Matched property sizing removes the break-even cliff, so it drops down
the list. The order now:

**Revised again after 5c.** Filling was the original headline risk and matched sizing demoted
it. Disintermediation replaced it and the management-not-introduction framing has now demoted
that too. Current order:

**1. Willingness to pay, now the top risk.** The business rests on teachers valuing relief from
infrastructure at roughly 27% of their retreat margin. Everyone says yes to being relieved; far
fewer say yes at $4,000. This is the one assumption that, if wrong, ends it, and it is the
cheapest thing on this list to test.

**2. Founder capacity.** Management is real work, so the ceiling is hours rather than demand.
Twenty to thirty hours a retreat caps a solo operator at roughly 20 to 30 a year. Growth past
that means hiring, and a different business.

**3. Disintermediation, much reduced.** If the product is management rather than introduction,
she needs you every time and the same house is not a leak. The risk returns only if the service
degrades into an introduction, which is what happens when the founder is overloaded. Note that
risks 2 and 3 are linked.

**4. Filling, reduced.** Per-head economics hold at six people, so no audience threshold
applies. What remains is teachers who cannot find even six, and teachers who will not attempt a
retreat at all.

**Venue-finding may still not be the bottleneck**, which is a separate matter from filling and
is what section 8 tests. If teachers say venues are easy, the service is a convenience rather
than a solution, and prices accordingly.

**Competition is real but shallow.** RetreatHub, RetreatVenues, Experience Retreats and Retreat
Me Happy all list Australian venues. They are SEO directories. None of them knows a single
teacher, and none can help fill.

## 8. The test, which costs nothing

**This section was rewritten after the founder-fit facts in 5a.** The original test was abstract
market research, which made sense when supply looked expensive to assemble. It is not, so the
better test is a real offer.

**Build one concrete Byron package and show it to five Melbourne teachers.** A named house, a
named caterer, real dates in a shoulder-season midweek window, a price per head, the number of
guests needed to break even, **and the management fee stated in dollars**. Not a concept, an
offer.

**The fee must be on the page.** Per 5c, a package without a price tests enthusiasm and every
teacher is enthusiastic about not doing admin. A package quoting $4,000 against her $16,400
margin tests the business. Warmth about the first is not evidence for the second, and
conflating them is the most likely way to talk yourself into this.

Read the responses:

* **Two ask how to book** and there is a business, tested with real money rather than opinion.
* **All five say it is lovely but they could not fill it** and the binding constraint is demand,
  exactly as section 7 warns. That is the single most valuable thing to learn and it is cheap.
* **Any of them say they would rather organise it themselves** and the disintermediation risk in
  section 6 is live from day one.

With warm Byron relationships this is roughly a week of work, not a project.

Two questions still worth asking in the same conversations, since they cost nothing:

1. To anyone who has run a retreat: *"Where did your guests come from?"* If the answer is her own
   students almost entirely, the overseas expansion is dead and the domestic case is confirmed.
2. *"How did you find the venue, and what did you settle for?"* The gap between what she wanted
   and what she booked is the product specification.

## 10. Assumptions, flagged so they can be challenged

1. **Deal-size figures in section 4 are estimates**, built from $180/person/night and
   $140/person/day. Not quoted from any Australian venue. Verify against three real quotes
   before relying on them.
2. **"2 to 5 percent of teachers run a retreat annually" is a guess**, as is the 2,000 to 4,000
   Melbourne teacher population. Both drive the ceiling calculation and neither is sourced.
3. **The claim that filling beats venue-finding as the bottleneck is an inference**, not
   measured. It is also the single most important assumption in this note, which is why
   section 8 tests it first.
4. **The overseas-fails argument rests on flight economics**, reasoned rather than measured, and
   it was initially over-applied. It holds for Europe and North America. It does **not** hold for
   Singapore, where the zero-domestic-capacity figure is verified but the willingness to pay an
   Australian premium over Bali is not.
5. **A second process note.** Perth and Margaret River were proposed as the Singapore gateway on
   desk reasoning about flight hours, and Mark corrected it from domain knowledge: the prime
   destinations are Byron, Noosa and Tasmania. The absence of a direct Singapore to Gold Coast
   service is verified; remaining flight times and durations in this note are general knowledge,
   not looked up, and should be confirmed before they inform a decision. The general lesson is
   recorded because it will recur: **desk reasoning optimises the legible variable and misses
   the market one.**
6. **Referral expansion between teacher communities is asserted from how those communities
   generally work**, not from YiM evidence.
7. **The whole-house figures in the premium section are estimates**, at $2,000 to $4,000 a night

## 11. What has not changed

**The editorial firewall is untouched and is not negotiable.** Two specific traps this idea
creates, both worth naming because both are the natural thing to reach for:

* **Never buy venue supply with coverage.** Cold-pitching a hotel, the obvious sentence is
  "list with us and we'll feature you." That is coverage as currency and it is exactly what the
  rule forbids. Venue supply is bought with a commercial proposition: qualified group bookings,
  midweek and shoulder-season fill. If a venue is worth writing about, write about it whether or
  not it pays, and if it pays, do not write about it differently.
* **A teacher paying for a service must not get different editorial treatment from one who does
  not.** Same rule as studio subscriptions, same discipline required.
* **New, and specific to this business: the pitch and the profile must never arrive in the same
  conversation.** Once Mark is brokering retreats to Melbourne teachers, the editorial subject
  list and the customer list overlap almost completely. A teacher who is approached for a
  profile and pitched a Byron package in the same breath will reasonably wonder which one was
  the real reason for the call, and she will tell other teachers. Separate the two by weeks and
  by context, and never pitch a teacher whose profile is unpublished or in progress.

**The firewall gets harder to hold from here, not easier.** Every previous revenue idea was
speculative. This one could work, and a working commercial venture next to an editorial
publication is precisely the situation the firewall exists for. The stronger the brokerage gets,
the more disciplined the separation has to be.

**The 15 Aug growth model still stands.** YiM grows by publishing through an expanding network,
not by profiling teachers one at a time. This note is consistent with it and sharpens one point:
**a teacher does not need a profile to be a customer.** The growth asset for this business is a
**teacher mailing list, not a teacher roster**. 400 teachers reading something useful monthly is
a plausible twelve-month goal and a $32,000 to $64,000 business; 400 profiles is not.

**Instagram is still the near-term channel** and the editorial launch is still the current
priority. Nothing here should pull time from finishing it.

**Sequencing is unchanged:** editorial launch, audience, attribution, commission, studio
subscriptions, multi-city licensing. This idea sits alongside that roadmap as an option that
happens to be available earlier than the commission step, not as a replacement for it.
